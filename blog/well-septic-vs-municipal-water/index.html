Well and Septic vs. Municipal Water: What to Know If You're Moving to Douglas County

A single distinction that changes ongoing maintenance responsibilities and costs.

By the Castle Rock Pro Plumbing team · Updated 2026

If you are relocating to Douglas County, one of the more consequential differences between properties you might not think to ask about upfront is whether a home is on municipal water and sewer or a private well and septic system. This single distinction changes ongoing maintenance responsibilities, costs, and what "normal" plumbing service even looks like.

Why This Varies So Much Within Douglas County

Most in-town Castle Rock addresses connect to municipal water through Castle Rock Water and municipal sewer through the same utility. Parker has its own municipal system through Parker Water and Sanitation District. But unincorporated areas throughout Douglas County, including the rural acreage properties near Castlewood Canyon and Franktown, are commonly served by private wells and septic systems instead. This is not a reflection of a particular area being underserved; it reflects genuine rural and semi-rural land use patterns where municipal infrastructure was never extended.

What Owning a Well Actually Involves

Water Quality Testing

Unlike municipal water, tested and reported by the utility, a private well owner is responsible for their own periodic water quality testing.

Pump Maintenance

The well pump is mechanical equipment with a service life, and its failure means a complete loss of water, not just reduced pressure.

Pressure Tank

Most well systems include a pressure tank that requires periodic inspection and eventual replacement.

No Monthly Utility Bill

You are not paying a water bill, but you are responsible for all maintenance costs directly.

What Owning a Septic System Actually Involves

A septic system requires periodic tank pumping, typically every three to five years depending on household size and usage, along with reasonable care about what goes down the drain. Harsh chemical drain cleaners, excessive garbage disposal use, and certain household chemicals can disrupt the bacterial balance a septic system depends on to function properly, in ways that simply do not matter the same way on municipal sewer. The drain field itself also needs protection from compaction, meaning parking or heavy equipment on that area of the yard is worth avoiding.

Cost Comparison: Not as Simple as It Looks

A well and septic property has no monthly water or sewer bill, which looks appealing on paper. In practice, the ongoing maintenance costs, periodic septic pumping, occasional well pump repairs or replacement, and water treatment if your well water needs it, often average out closer to a municipal bill than the sticker-price comparison suggests. The real difference is more about predictability: a municipal bill is a known monthly cost, while well and septic costs are more variable and can arrive as larger, less predictable expenses.

What to Check Before Buying a Well and Septic Property

  • Request recent well water quality test results, or have your own test performed.
  • Ask about well yield, meaning how much water the well actually produces, especially during dry periods.
  • Request septic tank pumping records and ask when the system was last inspected.
  • Confirm the septic system's designed capacity matches your household size.
  • Ask whether the property has ever experienced a well running dry or a septic backup.
  • Understand the location of both the well and the septic system and drain field on the property.

Getting Help With Either System

If you are moving into a well and septic property, our septic tank pumping service covers the maintenance side, and we can help establish a documented service history if the previous owner's records are incomplete. For municipal-connected properties throughout Parker and our other coverage areas, standard service applies.

Insurance and Financing Considerations

Some mortgage lenders require well water quality testing and septic system inspection as a condition of financing, separate from a standard home inspection. Building this into your closing timeline early avoids a last-minute scramble if a lender requirement surfaces later in the process. Homeowners insurance for well and septic properties is generally comparable to municipal-connected homes, though it is worth confirming with your specific insurer whether either system requires separate coverage consideration.

What Happens When a Well Runs Dry

A well running dry, whether from drought conditions or simply drawing more water than the aquifer can sustainably provide, is one of the more serious well ownership risks. This is why asking about well yield specifically, not just whether the well currently works, matters during a property evaluation. A well that barely meets current household demand leaves little margin if usage increases or a dry year reduces the water table.

Water Treatment Needs Are More Individual

Municipal water is treated centrally to a consistent standard across the entire service area. Well water quality varies significantly property to property, even between neighboring homes, depending on the specific aquifer depth and local geology. This means a water softener or additional treatment system that works well for one well and septic property may not be the right solution for another, making an actual water test far more important for a well property than it typically is for someone on municipal service.

Septic System Longevity and Replacement

A well-maintained septic system can last several decades, but eventual drain field replacement is a real possibility for an aging system, and it is a significant expense when it happens. Signs of a failing drain field include persistently soggy areas over it, unusually lush grass in that specific area even during dry conditions, and slow drains throughout the house despite a recently pumped tank. Catching this early, similar to a sewer line problem, generally means a less expensive intervention than waiting for full failure.

Whatever type of property you end up choosing, understanding these differences before you own the home means fewer surprises and a more realistic sense of what ongoing ownership actually involves.

If you already own a well and septic property and are unsure of your system's history or current condition, that is worth addressing now rather than waiting for a problem to force the issue.
