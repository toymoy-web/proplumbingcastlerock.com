Why Is My Water Bill So High?

Ruling out a hidden leak before assuming a billing error.

By the Castle Rock Pro Plumbing team · Updated 2026

A water bill that jumps for no obvious reason is one of the more unsettling things a homeowner can notice. Before assuming it is a billing error or a rate increase, it is worth ruling out a hidden leak, since a surprising number of unexplained bill spikes trace back to water running somewhere the homeowner cannot see.

Start With the Water Meter Test

This is the simplest way to check for a leak yourself. Make sure no water is being used anywhere in the house, no faucets running, no dishwasher or washing machine cycling, nothing. Then check your water meter. If the meter is still moving, or if it has a small triangular or star-shaped leak indicator that is spinning, water is flowing somewhere even though everything appears off.

Common Hidden Leak Locations

Toilets

A worn flapper can leak silently from tank to bowl, sometimes wasting significant water without any visible sign.

Underground Service Line

The line between the meter and the house can leak into the soil without ever surfacing visibly.

Under a Slab

A leak beneath a concrete foundation can run for a long time before a warm floor spot or other sign appears.

Water Heater

A slow leak from the tank or a fitting can go unnoticed if it drains toward a floor drain rather than pooling visibly.

Why This Comes Up More in Castle Rock

The expansive Pierre Shale and Denver Formation clay under much of Castle Rock swells and shrinks with moisture, putting uneven stress on buried water lines over time. That soil movement is a real, documented cause of underground leaks that never produce an obvious puddle, since the water simply disperses into the surrounding soil before reaching the surface. A home in an area with more of this clay movement is statistically more likely to develop this kind of leak than one on more stable ground.

A Simple Toilet Test

Toilets are the single most common source of a silent household leak. Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank, wait about ten minutes without flushing, and check the bowl. If color appears in the bowl, the flapper is leaking, an inexpensive repair. This test takes five minutes and rules out the most likely culprit before assuming something more serious.

When to Call a Professional

If the meter test confirms water is running somewhere and the toilet test comes back clean, the leak is likely hidden in a wall, under a slab, or on the underground service line. At that point, electronic leak detection is the right next step rather than guessing or, worse, opening walls or digging without knowing the actual location first.

Reading Your Bill History

Before assuming the worst, pull up your last several months of water bills if your utility provides that history online. A gradual, steady increase over many months looks different from a sudden jump in a single billing period. A sudden jump is more consistent with a new leak that started recently, while a gradual climb might point to a slowly worsening issue, a household habit change, or in some cases a seasonal factor like increased outdoor watering.

Comparing the same month year over year is also useful. If your bill this July is dramatically higher than last July with no real change in household water use, that comparison is a stronger signal than comparing July to a low-usage month like February.

Outdoor Leaks Are Easy to Miss

Not every leak is inside the house. An irrigation system with a cracked line, a leaking hose bib, or a break in the line running to a detached structure can all drive up usage without any visible sign indoors. If your property has a sprinkler system or outdoor water features, check those separately from the indoor meter test, since a leak isolated to an outdoor zone will not necessarily show up the same way.

What Electronic Leak Detection Actually Involves

When a leak is confirmed but not visible, a professional uses acoustic listening equipment that can detect the sound of water escaping under pressure through pipe material and soil. Combined with pressure testing and, where relevant, thermal imaging, this narrows the leak to a specific, confirmed location before anyone opens a wall, cuts into a slab, or starts digging in the yard. This is a meaningfully different approach from guessing based on where water eventually surfaces, which can be far from the actual source depending on soil type and pipe routing.

After the Leak Is Found

Once the location is confirmed, the repair approach depends on where it is. A leak in an accessible wall is usually the simplest fix. A leak under a slab may call for a decision between a direct repair at that spot or rerouting the line entirely, depending on the pipe's overall condition. A leak on the underground service line between the meter and the house is its own category, sometimes addressed with trenchless methods that avoid digging up the whole yard.

Whatever the location, the repair is quoted with a flat price after the leak is located, not before, since an accurate number depends on knowing exactly where and how bad the problem actually is.

If your own testing has confirmed something worth investigating, do not wait months hoping it resolves on its own. The sooner a hidden leak is found, the less total water and money it costs before the fix.
